## Summary (3 sentences)
The authors measure, with a past-only LightGBM forecaster and the user's requested walltime, how much of the carbon saving of a delay-to-lowest-integral planner survives deployment, for three public HPC traces replayed on 42 U.S. grids at 2, 6, 12 and 24 h slack. They find modest absolute savings (1.9, 3.8, 1.5 % of run-now emissions at 12 h), a minority to a half of the same planner's perfect-information saving, a runtime-estimate cost that exceeds the forecast cost on two of three traces by point estimate, and a recovery from a mean-of-last-two-jobs predictor. The study is careful about its scope (non-interruptible, no queue, average not marginal, foreign traces on U.S. grids) and reports negative baselines, but its simple-policy ladder and its operator-realisable walltime rules are not in the main results.

## Recommendation: MAJOR
The measurement is useful and honestly bounded; the weaknesses are the baselines and what the runtime term actually measures.

## Major comments
D-1. The runtime term is measured against the loosest possible input, and the main text does not yet show what an operator can do about it (Sections 4.2, 5.2; Fig 7; Table 4C). Median actual/requested ratios are 0.143, 0.306 and 0.178 (Table 2), so a planner that trusts R_req reserves a window "far longer than the job needs" (6.1). The Shapley runtime term is therefore a statement about how loose requests are, not about runtime information in general. The padded estimate, the quantile estimate and the X1 sweep exist (Section 4.2) but their results are pending or in the supplement (Section 5.7; Fig 13 empty). Fix: move padded, quantile and the X1 curve, with D and lgbm|pred, into one compact main-text panel (Table 4E or Fig 13) and rewrite the "recovery" claim (H3) as the share of the H2 penalty that a trivial operator rule removes (H3/H2 is 47, 35 and 27 % from Table 4B, which belongs in the text).
D-2. A forecast-free threshold rule is defined (p. 11) but no result for it appears anywhere in the main text. This is the baseline Sukprasert et al. motivate (simple policies capture most of the gain), and the brief puts it first (D1). The ladder in Table 4D is all forecast-based signals paired with R_req. Fix: add the threshold rule to Table 4D under R_req and to the pred row, and report "best simple policy minus D" with an interval; say plainly if D does not win (it may not, since D beats profile7|req by only 0.79 pp on PM100 and 0.47 pp on F-DATA).
D-3. What the forecaster adds is small and the design choice behind D is not well supported (Sections 4.3, 5.2). Test MAE at 1 to 24 h is 22.0 g/kWh for LightGBM against 24.0 for seasonal naive day, an 8 % improvement; D was chosen on DEV MAE counts (38 of 42 BAs) and the text itself says "an error rank does not imply a saving rank". The paper then still frames "forecast term" as a property of past-only forecasts in general. Fix: say in Section 5.2 that the forecast term is for this forecaster and that profile7 alone gives most of D's saving (3.75 against 4.08 % for the act runtime from the ledger, and 1.11 against 1.90 % for req); report the S5 tuned forecaster beside it or drop the S5 references (Section 6.4 K, Conclusion).
D-4. Counterfactual realism (Sections 3.2, 3.3, 6.4 A). Three foreign traces (2016 to 2018, 2020, 2021 and 2023) on 2025 to 2026 U.S. grids; arrivals placed at the original weekday and clock time in BA standard time (so summer arrivals can shift by one hour); one constant mean power per job; 20,000 jobs per BA drawn from 394, 1,257 and 165 users. At 5.5 h mean delay for 12 h slack on all jobs, a user-experience metric is missing: bounded slowdown or the share of short jobs delayed by hours. Fix: add delay by runtime class (a small table in Section 5.6) since Fig 10a already splits by job length.
