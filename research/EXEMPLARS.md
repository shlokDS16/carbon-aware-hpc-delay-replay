## 2. "They do X, we must do Y": bar items

1. Scale of regions and years. Sukprasert uses 123 regions and 3 years of hourly data; Wiesner uses 4 regions and 1 year. We must use at least 100 regions with open hourly data and at least 3 full years, or state why not.
2. Artifact release. Wiesner (GitHub), Sukprasert (Zenodo, GitHub, artifact appendix) and CarbonCast (GitHub) all release code and data. We must release code, derived data and a DOI-stamped Zenodo archive with a reproduce-all-figures script before submission.
3. Uncertainty. Of the SUSCOM papers I could assess (100857 full preprint; 100859 and 100856 abstracts), none reports intervals or tests; the carbon papers use confidence intervals or mean plus standard deviation over all start times (Sukprasert), 10 repeated runs (Wiesner) or 95 % bands (Radovanovic). We must report block-bootstrap confidence intervals over days, paired tests with multiplicity control where policies are compared, and the number of seeds.
4. Baselines. Every carbon paper compares against a carbon-agnostic baseline. We must include that baseline, simple policies (lowest-carbon slot within deadline, threshold rules) and an oracle with perfect forecasts, and report results next to all of them.
5. Negative results. Sukprasert finds simple policies capture most of the gain. We must test any sophisticated method against the best simple policy and say plainly if it does not win.
6. Forecast error. Wiesner injects 5 % error; Sukprasert has a prediction-error scenario; CarbonCast is itself a forecaster. We must use measured day-ahead forecast error, not only synthetic noise, and show sensitivity.
7. Workload realism. Wiesner assumes delay tolerance and Sukprasert uses configured job classes; Radovanovic uses proprietary data. We must use at least one public job trace (Google, Alibaba or Azure) beside synthetic classes and say which result depends on which.
8. Scenarios. Sukprasert includes what-if sections (mixed workloads, prediction error, increasing renewable penetration, combined spatial and temporal shifting). We must add a cleaner-grid scenario so the benefit is shown to shrink or grow with the mix, and a capacity-constrained case (Sukprasert's spatial bound assumes infinite capacity).
9. Novelty statement. The exemplars state one gap sentence plus a numbered contribution list. We must write one gap sentence backed by a related-work comparison table (our item versus Wiesner, Sukprasert, Radovanovic, CarbonCast) and a numbered contribution list.
10. Reporting of effects. 100859's abstract gives only mean percent gains over unnamed benchmarks. We must give absolute (kgCO2e) and relative effects with intervals and name every baseline.
11. Size of the paper. The carbon papers have 11 to 13 figures and 1 to 6 tables in about 9,000 to 13,500 words (arXiv text). We should plan about 10 to 12 figures, 4 to 6 tables and 9,000 to 11,000 words.
12. Titles. Of the 50 top SUSCOM 2023 to 2026 titles, 44 are descriptive noun phrases (mean 13.4 words overall) and 6 are Name-colon method titles; none is finding-led. The stronger carbon venues use descriptive titles that carry the object or the limit ("Limitations of ..."). A finding-led title would stand out and fits the project's title rule; keep it under about 18 words and name the object.
13. Prose. SUSCOM abstracts in my sample are 180 to 308 words, about 25 words per sentence, about one sentence in three passive, "we" for the contribution (STYLE_PROFILE.md). Write a 220 to 250 word abstract with numbers and intervals, one English variant throughout, no em or en dashes.
14. Evidence level. SUSCOM lane A is dominated by metaheuristic scheduling and offloading papers evaluated in simulators with unnamed or few baselines (ranks 7, 20, 33, 39, 42 in JOURNAL_BASICS, whose abstracts I could see only in part). We can clear that bar by measured, multi-region, real-trace evaluation with named baselines, but only the stronger-venue papers above show what that looks like, so aim for their standard, not the SUSCOM sample.
