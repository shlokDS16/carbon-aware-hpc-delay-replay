## Abstract

The Internet of Things (IoTs) has transformed the digital landscape by interconnecting billions of devices worldwide, paving the way for smart cities, homes, and industries. With the exponential growth of IoT devices and the vast amount of data they generate, concerns have arisen regarding efficient task-offloading strategies. Traditional cloud and edge computing methods, paired with basic Machine Learning (ML) algorithms, face several challenges in this regard. In this paper, we propose a novel approach to task offloading in a Device-toDevice (D2D)-Edge-Cloud computing using the Rainbow Deep Q-Network (DQN), an advanced Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) algorithm. This algorithm utilizes advanced neural networks to optimize task offloading in the three-tier framework. It balances the trade-offs among D2D, Device-to-Edge (D2E), and Device/ Edge-to-Cloud (D2C/E2C) communications, benefiting both end users and servers. These networks leverage Deep Learning (DL) to discern patterns, evaluate potential offloading decisions, and adapt in real time to dynamic environments. We compared our proposed algorithm against other state-of-the-art methods. Through rigorous simulations, we achieved remarkable improvements across key metrics: an increase in energy efficiency by 29.8%, a 27.5% reduction in latency, and a 43.1% surge in utility.

The challenge of task offloading in a D2D-Edge-Cloud computing system requires a robust decision-making mechanism to ensure optimal energy consumption, latency, and communication overhead. Rainbow DQN [30], an enhanced version of the standard DQN, offers a potent solution by amalgamating several advancements from the realm of DRL. At the heart of the Rainbow DQN is the Double DQN (DDQN) mechanism [31]. In traditional DQNs, there's a tendency to overestimate Q-values, which can lead to suboptimal offloading decisions. This overestimation might result in tasks being offloaded when it's not energy-efficient to do so or might increase the latency due to poor server selection. DDQN addresses this by decoupling the action selection from its evaluation. It achieves this by employing two networks: one for selecting the best action (the online network) and another for evaluating the chosen action (the target network). In the context of our system, this means that the IoT device, when deciding where to offload a task, gets a more accurate estimation of the outcomes of its actions, ensuring energy-efficient decisions that also optimize for latency. Next, the Prioritized Experience Replay mechanism ensures that the network learns more efficiently [32]. Traditional DQNs sample experiences (or transitions) uniformly from the memory. However, not all experiences contribute equally to the learning process. Some experiences, especially those where the network's predictions were off by a large margin, offer more learning value. By prioritizing the sampling of such experiences based on their Temporal Difference (TD) error, the network can focus on the most informative experiences. For task offloading, this means the system will quickly learn from any poor offloading decisions, adapting its strategy to prevent similar mistakes in the future. The Dueling DQN architecture further refines the decision-making process [33]. Instead of estimating the Q-value for each state-action pair directly, it splits the estimation into two streams: one for the state value function and another for the action advantage function. This separation allows the network to learn the value of being in a particular state without being clouded by the effects of each action. In the offloading scenario, this could help the IoT device understand the inherent benefits or challenges of its current situation (e.g., battery level, network congestion) before deciding on the best offloading strategy. In the task offloading scenario using Rainbow DQN, here's a step-by-step breakdown of the process:

Observation: The IoT device observes its current state, which includes its status, the nature of tasks awaiting offloading, and the conditions of the edge servers and cloud.

Decision: Based on its learned policy and current state, the IoT device uses the Rainbow DQN to decide where to offload the task ' this could be another device, an edge server, or the cloud.

Action and Feedback: Upon deciding, the IoT device offloads the task. It then receives feedback in terms of energy consumed, latency experienced, and any communication overhead.

Learning: This feedback is used to compute the reward or penalty. The experience (state, action, reward, next state) is stored in the memory. The Rainbow DQN then samples from this memory, prioritizing experiences with high TD errors, and updates its weights to improve its policy.

The objective is to minimize the energy consumption, latency, and communication overhead while ensuring the computational requirements are met. The cumulative reward R for device i is given by Eq. (35).

## Simulation environment and parameters

Our simulation environment was designed to capture the complexities of a D2D-Edge-Cloud system, focusing on the intricate interactions between IoT devices, edge nodes, and cloud servers. This setup allows for a comprehensive evaluation of the task offloading mechanisms proposed in our research. The simulated network includes a variable number of IoT devices (100, 200, or 300), with each device having computational capabilities ranging from 1 to 3 GHz. These devices generate tasks with data bits ranging from 0.2 to 4 MB and computation demands from 0.2 to 4 GHz. The tasks can be offloaded to the available edge nodes or directly to the cloud servers. 10 edge nodes are strategically placed within the network, each with a communication radius of 300 m and computing capabilities varying between 6 and 15 GHz. These edge nodes are responsible for handling offloaded tasks, with available computing resources ranging from 0.2 to 0.5 F'm. The edge nodes can further offload tasks to any of the three cloud servers if necessary. Communication between IoT devices can occur within a radius of 30 m, allowing for D2D interactions. The transmission rates for IoT-to-edge and edge-to-cloud communications are set between 2 and 3 Mb/s and 0.8'1.5 Mb/s, respectively. Detailed information and parameters are mentioned in Table 3.

## Conclusion and future works

In this paper, we have introduced a comprehensive task offloading framework within the D2D-Edge-Cloud environment, leveraging the capabilities of the Rainbow DQN, a sophisticated variant of DRL. Our approach stands out in its ability to make intelligent offloading decisions across the three tiers, optimizing the balance between D2D, D2E, and D2C/E2C communications. This not only enhances the performance of end devices but also optimizes the utilization of edge and cloud resources. Our extensive simulations and comparative analysis with stateof-the-art algorithms have demonstrated significant improvements in energy efficiency, latency, and utility, underscoring the efficacy of our proposed solution. However, there are some limitations to our current framework. The computational complexity introduced by the Rainbow DQN, which may lead to increased overhead, requires careful consideration. Additionally, the dependency on consistent and high-quality data for training the DRL model poses challenges in environments where data may be noisy or sparse. The real-world deployment of our framework could also face limitations in terms of infrastructure and technology variability across different regions. Moreover, the integration of security and privacy measures in the offloading process has not been deeply explored in our current framework. To overcome the limitations identified in our current framework, future research will be directed toward optimizing the computational efficiency of the Rainbow DQN algorithm. Furthermore, future work will focus on adapting the framework to the diverse infrastructural and technological contexts found in real-world deployments. Moreover, introducing robust security and privacy measures in the task offloading process, while maintaining efficiency, is a challenge we are eager to tackle. By addressing these areas, we aim to push our framework closer to real-world applicability and broader adoption in the IoT landscape.
