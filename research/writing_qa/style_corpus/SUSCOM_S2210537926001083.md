## Abstract

The rapid rise of cloud computing and edge services has dramatically increased the carbon footprint associated with digital infrastructure, leading to calls for new solutions to aid in the sustainable management of resources. This paper proposes a unique carbon-aware workload orchestrator for federated edge-cloud systems that balances carbon emissions, energy costs, service-level agreement (SLA) violations, and resource utilization with a dynamic spatio-temporal workload management approach. The proposed approach incorporates adaptive weighting based on real-time carbon intensity, federation constraints, and multi-domain resource optimization, and address critical gaps in existing carbon-aware scheduling methods. We show results of a detailed simulation with 3969 unique workloads over 7 days across 3 cloud data centers and 9 edge nodes, where the proposed algorithm showed statistically significant improvement over static scheduling baselines, including a 10.2% reduction in carbon emissions (? < 0.001), a 15.7% reduction in costs (? < 0.001), and a relative reduction of SLA violations of 60% (? < 0.001), and achieved a 14.0% improvement in carbon efficiency. The framework's effective scalability has been established through a complete study with larger federations. The orchestrator uses intelligent decision-making that modifies operation according to real-time and local environmental conditions while determining the level of operational efficiency across federated domains. An additional analysis of the framework's ability to guarantee fairness for each workload type has been identified through a head-to-head competition against state-of-the-art approaches, showing improvement across the majority of metrics, including significant improvement in fairness (21.6%) and eliminating SLA violations for the emergency workload type. The work shows the critical importance of combined carbon awareness in resource provisioning for sustainable cloud'edge computing and presents an approach for environmentally conscious workload provisioning across distributed systems.

## Related work

Carbon-aware computing has developed as an impactful research domain that addresses the ecological consequences of the use of digital infrastructure. The evolution of carbon-aware computing methods has shifted from elementary approaches to improving energy efficiency to a formalized method for developing multi-objective optimization approaches. The literature within the domain can generally be characterized as involving spatial shifting approaches, temporal shifting approaches, or hybrid approaches that involve spatial and temporal shifting, incorporating more advanced Artificial Intelligence (AI) techniques within these problem settings. In addition to this literature, traditional approaches to scheduling, such as static scheduling [10,11] and energy-aware scheduling [12,13], can be referenced as baseline approaches in outcomes from carbon-aware algorithms. Static scheduling approaches tend to fix the assignment of workloads in a manner without consideration of the dynamic nature of the other parts of the system that can be optimized. Energy-aware scheduling summarizes and implements approaches to the problem of reducing energy consumption without carbon intensity as the core consideration.

Spatial shifting approaches take advantage of geographical differences in carbon intensities, which are associated with the movement of workloads to other locations with cleaner energy sources. The carbon footprint of your energy generation varies greatly across locations. Radovanovic et al. [14] were the first to select data centers based on carbon emissions through their work on carbon-aware computing for data centers, showing that simply shifting loads geographically can cause substantial emissions reductions while still meeting service level objectives. They showed that simply redistributing workloads between datacenters that are geographically dispersed could yield substantial carbon reductions and maintain application performance. Continuing this work, Zhao et al. [15] extended Radovanovic et al.'s work with adaptive overload detection and virtual machine consolidation techniques while using dynamic threshold adjustment methods to avoid violating service level agreements while minimizing energy consumption. Rawas et al. [16] also suggested LECC, a model that considered location, energy, carbon, and cost in the decision-making process for virtual machines, furthering the complexity of the resource management problem across more characteristics. It is, however, important to note that the above approaches often disregard the additional network overhead and latency due to large geographical shifts, and this is particularly relevant in edge computing, where the user generates requests based on locality.

Temporal shifting strategies adjust the timing of workload execution with the intention of executing during moments of lower carbon intensity, typically correlated with high renewable generation, and occurring at specific times of the day or under specific weather conditions. Wiesner et al. [9] introduced the unique ''Let's Wait Awhile'' method and systematically demonstrated the significant impact of delayed execution for carbon reduction in cloud environments. Their work offered important information on the carbon-saving potential of different workload types and was based on the ''shiftability'' of different load types. Lin and Chien [17] explored adapting datacenter capacity in relation to the carbon intensity of the grid and developed models to align computational demand and availability of clean energy. More recently, Beena et al. [18] incorporated forecasting carbon intensity with task scheduling in heterogeneous cloud environments, obtaining considerable energy efficiency and reductions in carbon. Though strongly effective for workloads that can be delayed, temporal shifting methods generally do not accommodate latency-sensitive applications that require immediate execution, thus creating an impetus for hybrid methods that can adapt to and bind workloads within these dual considerations.

To develop a broad context for our comparative analysis, we consider original scheduling algorithms that map the evolutionary starting point of resource management for distributed systems.

The static scheduling approaches embody the traditional methods employed in resource allocation, featuring predetermined policies that do not account for dynamic system conditions or environmental conditions. Static scheduling approaches include elementary heuristics such as round-robin, first-come'first-served, and fixed-priority allocations that do not factor in real-time carbon intensity, variance in energy pricing, or work criticality [10,11]. Although static scheduling approaches are computationally efficient and easy to implement, the unresponsiveness to changing environmental conditions creates an inefficient usage of both carbon emissions and the economy within the federated edge-cloud environments that exist today. Static schedulers are subject to rules and fixed resource mappings, which makes them incapable of accessing the opportunities for carbon reduction presented through spatio-temporal optimization of workloads.

Energy-aware scheduling algorithms were notable advances that prioritized reducing energy consumption in real time through one of several optimization mechanisms. This typically involved the use of an energy efficiency metric known as Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE), in addition to the incorporation of DVFS (dynamic voltage and frequency scaling) and power management to reduce electrical consumption [12,13]. Energy-aware scheduling metrics would typically reduce energy use by consolidating workloads, resulting in fewer active servers. Nevertheless, energy-aware scheduling metrics did not often account for carbon intensity explicitly, nor did they incorporate a sufficient level of multi-objective optimization across environmental, economic, and performance objectives. While there has been some movement towards improved practices from energy-aware scheduling algorithms to static algorithms, energy-aware algorithms still focus on energy quantity rather than energy carbon quality and, therefore, do not fully capitalize on the carbon reduction capacity generated by the proper use of spatio-temporal workload shifting in a federated geographically distributed environment.

The transition from traditional static and energy-efficient approaches to advanced AI-based strategies is a transformative advancement in the area of sustainable computing. Static schedulers [10, 11] provide rudimentary resource allocation, while energy-efficient methods [12,13] focus on operational efficiency, without the employment of predictive intelligence and multi-objective optimization required for carbon-aware computing in a federated context. The restrictions of static and energy-efficient approaches fueled the development of hybrid and AI-based approaches to manage complex trade-offs between environmental sustainability, economic efficiency, and quality of service.

In recent years, advances have been made that, among other fields utilize artificial intelligence for predictive optimization and multiobjective decision-making and represent state-of-the-art research within carbon-aware computing. Within their comprehensive landscape of carbon-aware cloud computing, Alex et al. [6] combined deep Qnetworks with agglomerative clustering for virtual machine placement using a CARBON-DQN algorithm that utilized reinforcement learning to adapt to a dynamic cloud environment while optimizing for emissions and energy consumption through intelligent resource consolidation. Siddique [7] developed a scheduling approach based on reinforcement learning that would integrate carbon intensity prediction with dynamic workload scheduling, which would allow for improved operational carbon intensity by as much as forty-two percent, while maintaining service quality. Addressing both operational and embodied emissions, Asadov et al. [4] proposed a comprehensive spatio-temporal algorithm

The CARBON-DQN approach [6] exemplifies the significant possibilities of deep reinforcement learning for carbon-aware scheduling but is mainly limited to centralized cloud systems, likely rendering it inapplicable to a federated edge-cloud framework. Our study builds on this work and operationalizes this work for federated edge-cloud environments while particularly incorporating factors such as domain limitations/constraints, adaptive weighting, and making real-time decisions that are vital in distributed frameworks. As well, CASPER [8] provides carbon-aware provisioning for distributed web services, significantly reducing emissions as well, yet it does not integrate federation awareness and dynamic adaptation coupled with changing environmental factors. The increasing consciousness of hardware manufacturers regarding embodied emissions has been noted by Asadov et al. [4] and Son et al. [19] in another area of carbon-aware computing that we start to engage with comprehensive lifecycle viewpoints.

In addition to the hybrid and AI-based approaches mentioned previously, a number of more recent publications (2023'2025) have advanced the state of the art, particularly in utilizing deep reinforcement learning (DRL) for carbon-aware scheduling. Kristiansson et al. [20] created a carbon-conscious scheduler of cyber'physical systems that run across the edge-cloud continuum. Their strategy makes a trade-off among energy consumption, real-time carbon intensity, and resource availability by adjusting workloads over time and across space. Although practical in their environment, the approach fails to address federation constraints such as data sovereignty or cross-domain policies, which we focus on in the proposed framework. However, the five-stage orchestration model introduced by Shingne et al. [21] would incorporate the performance of the combination of spatio-temporal energy prediction, quantum-inspired optimization, and thermal dynamics. It is impressive that they reduce their carbon footprint by 25%, yet their pipeline is not easy to adapt in real time due to its complexity. Conversely, our framework keeps decision-making lightweight and deterministic, which is more appropriate for an online scheduling context in dynamically federated settings.

Another research work relevant to DRL is the extension of CARBONDQN proposed by Alex et al. [6], which leverages agglomerative clustering to enhance the location of virtual machines. Their work is rather efficient in carbon at a centralized cloud database but presupposes only one administrative domain and does not consider a spatio-temporal shifting. Hogade et al. [22] introduced a game-theoretic DRL technique that minimizes both carbon and energy expenses associated with the AI inference workloads on geo-distributed data center networks. Their multi-agent model is innovative, although the training cost is high, and the scheme lacks adaptive weighting for various levels of workloads according to their importance. To avoid excessive training, our method employs a priority-based heuristic that has dynamic weights, which is more realistic in the context of federated deployments in the real world.

Recently, federated learning (FL) has been discussed as a solution to allow making carbon-sensitive decisions without violating data privacy. The Carbon-Conscious Federated Reinforcement Learning (CCFRL) proposed by Seo and Elmroth [23] has a maximum impact of reducing carbon emissions, up to 64%, in the cloud environments through having distributed agents share model updates only. Their proposed work is promising towards the use of privacy-sensitive applications, yet the emphasis of their work is on the training of machine learning models instead of managing real-time workloads with SLA deadlines. Also, Li et al. [24] made FedCarbon a green edge AI framework that has double flexible controls. Once again, the most important objective is the facility

training of energy-efficient models, not online scheduling. A different approach was chosen by Saad et al. [25], where FL has been used for a prediction of workload energy consumption in container orchestration, retaining sensitive operational data on a per-enterprise basis. Despite its importance, their work does not consider the shifting of workloads across federated domains in the spatio-temporal sense. This is the core difference of our framework: we apply federated principles only to impose domain constraints (e.g., data sovereignty), not model training. This enables us to arrive at quick, deterministic scheduling choices without the overhead of distributed learning.

An emerging body of literature acknowledges that the carbon emission during operation is not the full story, embodied carbon resulting from hardware manufacturing, transportation, and disposal is also important. A comprehensive approach to designing sustainable edge data centers, integrating the consideration of embodied carbon into the design and operation stages, was produced by Panteleaki et al. [26]. Their work is thorough, but it is offline and not developed to accommodate real-time workload placement. Yang et al. [27] proposed the idea of joint carbon-latency optimization in online service function chain implementation, explicitly considering amortized embodied and operational carbon. Their model of optimization is efficient but fails to deal with federation constraints or dynamic weighting when dealing with multiple objectives. Radovanovic et al. [14] also developed a carbon-intelligent computing system that relocates the workloads of the datacenter conditioned by forecasts of carbon intensity, a pioneering real-world implementation. Nonetheless, they have their centralized system and do not take care of multi-domain edge-cloud federation as we do. GreenScale [19] is another valuable contribution, as it offers a model of embodied carbon emissions that takes into consideration the lifetime of the server and server usage. Their model is interesting; however, the scheduler that accompanies it does not yet have adaptive weighting or federation awareness (see Table 1).

Four major new characteristics that deal with critical gaps in literature characterize the framework suggested in this research. First, we provide full federation awareness, including data sovereignty and domain trust constraints, enabling secure deployment across multiple administrative domains. Second, a dynamic adaptive weighting mechanism adjusts priorities in real time based on workload criticality and carbon intensity, rather than using fixed weights. Third, we integrate multi-objective optimization that explicitly handles trade-offs among carbon, cost, performance and SLA compliance. Fourth, the algorithms are designed with clearly stated constraints to ensure feasibility in heterogeneous federated edge-cloud scenarios. Recent advances in DRLbased orchestration, federated learning for carbon management, and lifecycle-aware scheduling ' while valuable ' do not simultaneously offer integrated spatio-temporal optimization, federation constraints, or dynamic adaptive weighting. Our framework fills this gap by combining all three features into a unified, practical orchestrator validated on realistic federated edge-cloud infrastructures.

Day-ahead vs. Real-time pricing . Most deregulating electricity markets set energy prices via day-ahead auctions based on load forecasts and generator bids, while real-time markets address any residual imbalances. Zhang et al. [28] have developed a joint forecasting model which captures local-temporal and cross-variable dependencies to enhance the prediction of day-ahead carbon intensity. These can be used to pre-position loads or spare capacity during low-carbon times. The prediction layer (Section 4.1) of our framework is designed to accommodate forward-looking forecasts, but our current evaluation is relying on real-time pricing for simplicity.

Renewable energy certificates and carbon offsets. Most cloud providers buy renewable energy certificates (RECs) or carbon offsets to assert renewable energy use or be carbon neutral. Although these tools can lower the reported carbon footprint of an organization, they do not alter the actual marginal carbon intensity of the consumed electricity. Our model is based on the location-based accounting approach of the Greenhouse Gas (GHG) Protocol [29]; the actual grid emissions where the consumption occurs are considered, not market-based accounting that incorporates RECs. This decision implies our focus on carbon reduction in real time.

Time-of-use tariffs and demand response. In certain regions, there are time-of-use (TOU) or demand response electricity rates that encourage load shifting to off-peak times. Ukwuoma et al. [30] demonstrated that TOU tariffs can be an excellent way to assist in decarbonization by matching the energy consumption with the availability of renewable generation. These mechanisms are complementary to carbon-conscious temporal shifting since out-of-peak times tend to be combined with reduced carbon intensity. The temporal shifting logic may be applied to TOU price signals, which we identify as a next step towards future research.

In order to make our carbon model compatible with established accounting frameworks, we based it on the Greenhouse Gas (GHG) Protocol Scope 2 guidelines [29], dealing with emissions from purchased electricity.

Marginal vs. Average carbon intensity. Our model uses average carbon intensity ???(?), defined as the total grid emissions divided by total electricity generation in that region. This is consistent with operational carbon accounting for existing data centers. However, recent work argues for marginal carbon intensity ' the emissions associated with the next unit of electricity demand ' which better captures the causal impact of workload scheduling decisions. Sukprasert et al. [31] systematically compared average and marginal signals in carbon-aware optimization, concluding that the choice significantly affects both short-term operational and long-term grid-level outcomes. While marginal intensity is more accurate for demand response, it requires detailed knowledge of generator dispatch curves. We use average intensity for its availability and stability, noting that the choice affects absolute emission calculations but not relative comparisons between schedulers.

Location-based vs. Market-based accounting . As noted above, our model adopts location-based accounting, which reflects the actual emissions intensity of the grid where energy is consumed. This contrasts with market-based accounting, which allows organizations to claim emissions reductions through REC purchases. Our focus on locationbased accounting aligns with the goal of real-time operational carbon reduction and is consistent with recent carbon-aware scheduling literature [8,14].

Operational vs. Embodied emissions. Consistent with the scope stated in Section 4, our current model addresses operational carbon emissions from electricity consumption. Embodied emissions from hardware manufacturing, transportation, and disposal are acknowledged as an important consideration (see Section 4) but are not quantitatively incorporated. This is a recognized limitation and a direction for future work, as noted in Sections 4 and 6.

In this study, we present the carbon-aware federated orchestration problem as a multi-objective optimization that seeks to maximize, for example, environmental sustainability, economic efficiency, and quality of service across federated domains. The multi-objective formulation recognizes that practical scheduling decisions are made in the real world and have to optimize against multiple, potentially conflicting, objectives rather than being solely optimized against a single parameter in isolation.

Achieving this goal is meaningful for environmental sustainability, as it reduced the emissions associated with compute workloads, which is part of worldwide climate aspirations and corporate sustainability goals.

Quality of use of service is critical to achieving user satisfaction, and continued quality service especially applies to workloads that sensitive to latency, work in emergencies, or emergent work.

This generic mathematical formulation captures the domainagnostic fundamental trade-offs and constraints inherent to carbonaware federated orchestration and provides a strong foundation for the solution approach proposed.

The novel carbon-aware federated orchestrator presented in this paper features a multi-layered architecture to overcome the challenges associated with sustainable resource management in distributed edgecloud settings. Specifically, this architecture integrates monitoring, prediction, decision-making, and execution across a federated architecture that promotes sustainable tradeoffs between environmental, financial, and performance goals.

The monitoring layer continuously collects a stream of data from the federated infrastructure, including carbon intensity data from locations around the world, real-time energy prices, resource consumption at the computing nodes, and workload characteristics. This data is crucial to the decision-making of the framework, which underpins the systems ability to respond to the carbon intensity and other environmental condition changes, and also build on the opportunities to optimize carbon. The monitoring part of the federated orchestrator explicitly includes components to monitor carbon intensity using platforms that include the Kubernetes Carbon Intensity Exporter from Microsoft [32], however, extends the work appropriately to tackle the context of federated edge-cloud applications.

The prediction layer takes advantage of sophisticated time-series forecasting approaches for predicting future conditions that will impact carbon-aware scheduling decisions. In particular, this involves predicting patterns of carbon intensity using historical data, forecasts of weather patterns, and projections of renewable generation, in addition to forecasting patterns of workload arrivals and demands for resources. The approach uses workload-based prediction and power-based prediction methods that leverage different benefits of each method as described in the comprehensive review by Asadov et al. [4]. The prediction models rely on Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks that can capture complex temporal dependencies in carbon intensity and workload patterns to inform scheduling decisions to support computational activities as energy produced at low-carbon intensity becomes available to reduce emissions associated with performing work.

This layer takes into consideration the possible workload application options through a variety of dimensions (e.g., carbon emissions, cost of energy, impact on performance and SLA compliance). This weighting mechanism has three modes that are activated depending on the states induced under a given situation condition. The specific weight values of each given mode are indicative of the relativity of the established set of objectives determined by the simulation analysis and relative experience.

In emergency conditions, where workloads involve serious deadline constraints or vital requirements, the weight mode prioritizes SLA compliance and performance to allow important workloads to meet their critical deadlines. The mode signifies that not all applications are able to bear a lot of delay or performance loss, even in cases where there are opportunities to reduce carbon. Such varying amounts of weight in this mode make sure that those workloads that are most important during an emergency scenario receive the necessary resources and priority and still manage to take moderate care of the environmental effects.

In cases where the electric grid is operating under high carbon intensity conditions or there are higher emissions factors, the focus of the strategy is on carbon reduction by increasing the weight assigned to environmental objectives. This method permits the system to actively avoid using compute resources during peak carbon intensity, either by temporally shifting usage into subsequent timeframes or spatially shifting use to locations with less carbon intensity. This concept is scientifically grounded in studies by both Asadov et al. [4] and Siddique [7], which seek to capitalize on the carbon reduction potential that exists with workload shifting into timeframes and locations with less carbon intensity during periods of high carbon intensity.

In normal operating conditions, the system ultimately exhibits balanced behavior, weighing all objectives without giving too much weight or emphasis on any particular dimension. This approach, as an operating mode, will provide reasonable performance in carbon reductions, cost reductions and service quality, thus this choice can be described as a sustainable operation in normal operating conditions. The actual weight numbers that were used for the standard operating mode were based upon the relative importance, which was determined from multiple simulation experiments and practical experience of operations.

The adaptability of the weighting approach will observe workload attributes and environmental characteristics to switch from one operating mode to another in order to support rapid and contextual decision support for optimal overall performance across multiple objectives.

The query recognizes that legal or regulatory restrictions may limit certain options when placing resources regardless of their ecological or economic completeness and applicability, thus modeling complex operational constraints of real-world systems when resource allocation is implemented in federation settings.

Several key principles of carbon-aware federated orchestration are realized in this algorithm. The prioritizing of workloads, for instance, allows urgent and time-critical workloads to be scheduled first, thus addressing essential service needs. The organizational sequence for assessing candidate nodes and start times allows for a reasonable and systematic exploration of the solution space while still satisfying practical constraints. The continual updating of weightings allows the system to respond to changing situations and needs by balancing multiple objectives according to the current priorities. The updated tracking of resource utilization continually maintains sound updated system state information for scheduling.

This work presents a holistic architecture for carbon-aware federated orchestration in edge-cloud architectures that addresses the most fundamental issue of sustainable resource management in distributed digital infrastructures in the context of sustainable development. Our research demonstrates that intelligent multi-objective optimization and dynamic spatiotemporal workload placement can generate considerable environmental and economic value while achieving high servicelevel quality and remaining within critical federation constraints. The suggested orchestrator in this study represents a significant advancement over existing state-of-the-art mechanisms, especially with respect to adaptive weighting, federation-aware workload placement, and spatiotemporal integrated shifting.

The main contributions of this research were rigorously evaluated through extensive simulations. The findings confirmed that our framework realized a statistically significant decrease of 10.2% in carbon emissions and a decrease of 15.7% in energy costs in relation to static scheduling baselines, and it was able to maintain 60% fewer SLA violations. Additionally, the proposed schedules achieved a 14.0% increase in total carbon efficiency, while also increasing fairness in the distribution of cloud resources to applications. Importantly, these policies are demonstrated to be implemented with full compliance with data sovereignty restrictions and operating domain limits; therefore, the practicality of adoption can be demonstrated in practice in occupying federated systems. The economic analysis provided a compelling case for adoption, with a payback of only 1.1 years, as well as sufficient operational cost savings to demonstrate that the consumption of carbon is aligned with an organization's economic outcome. Analytical insights reveal zero SLA violations for emergency workloads and highest carbon efficiency for batch tasks; temporal shifting gains are largest in regions with high diurnal carbon variability.

The effectiveness of our methodology arises from several interrelated design principles. First, the dynamic adaptive weighting mechanism facilitates context-aware decision making, prioritizing carbon emissions when grid intensity is high while maintaining service quality when satisfying emergency workloads. Second, the more comprehensive multi-objective formulation does not result in the less than optimal trade-offs of a single metric optimization while balancing environmental, economic and performance goals with a holistic approach. Third, the inclusion of federation constraints in the scheduling logic is essential and has not been addressed in earlier literature considering modern multi-domain edge-cloud infrastructures. Finally, time complexity is linear in the number of workloads, nodes, and deadline window. Realtime tests with up to 192 nodes show decision latency of 258 ms and communication overhead below 11 KB, confirming scalability.

In the future, this work opens various research directions. First, while we consider real-time carbon intensity in our model, adding predictive forecasting of the renewable energy generation would facilitate a more proactive scheduling framework. This enables moving

from optimizing process based on current situation to future situation, anticipating low carbon windows in the future while possibly improving efficiency. Second, as the carbon footprint of operational electricity goes down, the relative contribution of embodied emissions from hardware manufacturing becomes a larger portion of total emissions. A logical next step is to create a quantitative model to bring lifecycle emissions into the optimization objective, towards a genuine holistic carbon accounting system. Third, for the orchestrator to appear useful and operational, there will need to be credible and sophisticated strategy for handling uncertainty in the network. Thus, a priority will be to implement and validate the proposed approach in a physical federated testbed, and assess the performance of the approach in the context of real network latency, bandwidth, and level of partial failure. Finally, testing more sophisticated deep reinforcement learning methods would support the decision-making process on a truly large scale and dynamic federation.
