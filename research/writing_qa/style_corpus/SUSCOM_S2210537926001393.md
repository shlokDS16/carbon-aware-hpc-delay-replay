## Abstract

The rapid growth of data centers driven by artificial intelligence and cloud computing has intensified both electricity consumption and carbon emissions, necessitating integrated cost'carbon optimization strategies. This study proposes a comprehensive energy management framework for a photovoltaic (PV) and battery energy storage system (BESS) supported data center incorporating heterogeneous workload flexibility under thermal constraints and time-of-use tariffs. Three representative workload flexibility classes'short-running deferrable (Mode A), long-running continuous (Mode B), and long-running interruptible (Mode C)'are mathematically formulated and embedded into a unified PV'BESS thermal-aware energy magement system (EMS) framework that jointly minimizes grid electricity cost, battery degradation cost, and cooling system ageing cost while evaluating carbon performance. Results demonstrate that coordinated workload shifting significantly reduces peak-hour grid dependency and cooling demand, yielding substantial operational savings. In the fully combined Mode (A+B+C) configuration, total daily cost is reduced from 658 USD to 492 USD when BESS and shifting are jointly applied. From an environmental perspective, average Carbon Usage Effectiveness (CUE) improves from 0.455 to 0.349 kgCO?/kWhIT , Carbon-Free Energy (CFE) score increases from 66.2% to 72.7%, and avoided emissions rise from 2574 kgCO? to 3325 kgCO? under T'rkiye-specific grid parameters. The findings demonstrate that integrating computational flexibility, storage, and thermal-aware optimization transforms data centers into cost-efficient, low-carbon energy hubs capable of supporting renewable integration and grid stability.

## Introduction

Recent industrial reports highlight that energy management in data centers has become a strategic priority driven by the rapid growth of artificial intelligence (AI) workloads and ambitious climate targets. According to Google's 2025 Environmental Report [1], data center electricity consumption increased by 27% in 2024, while operational emissions were reduced by 12% through improved energy management and renewable energy integration. Google aims to operate its data centers using carbon-free energy on a 24/7 basis by 2030, and the global carbon-free energy (CFE) percentage increased from 64% to 66% in 2024. In nine out of twenty grid regions where Google operates, the CFE share has already exceeded 80%. These developments demonstrate that modern data centers are no longer passive energy consumers but active participants in power systems.

Large-scale data center operators increasingly deploy carbon-aware computing platforms that shift computational workloads to periods or locations with higher renewable energy availability. In addition, data centers can support grid operation by reducing electricity consumption during peak demand periods through coordinated operation with grid operators. Such capabilities position data centers as flexible energy resources that can contribute to grid stability and renewable energy integration [2].

The rapid growth of data centers has significantly increased electricity demand worldwide, creating substantial pressure on distribution networks during peak periods. At the same time, the integration of onsite renewable generation and battery energy storage systems (BESS) has transformed data centers into flexible energy hubs capable of adapting their electricity consumption patterns [3].

## Related work

Data centers hold significant potential to provide scalable and adaptable flexibility to modern power systems by utilizing their existing infrastructure and operational capabilities. Through flexible load management and on-site energy resources, data centers can support renewable energy integration and contribute to improving grid stability. Recent studies have demonstrated that data centers are promising candidates for demand response applications due to their controllable electricity consumption and inherent flexibility assets. Motivated by this potential, Takci et al. [5] propose a practical demand-response-based energy management framework for data centers that enables coordinated scheduling of grid power consumption and battery operation under realistic electricity tariff structures, including BESS, uninterruptible power supply (UPS) units, diesel generators, and thermal energy storage components associated with cooling systems such as cooling towers, pumps, chillers, and economizers. For instance, Lindberg et al. [6] proposed a geographic load shifting model for data centers to reduce carbon emissions based on locational marginal carbon emission signals. The approach uses DC optimal power flow to evaluate the impact of load shifting on system carbon emissions and determines optimal load shifts between geographically distributed data centers. Liu et al. [7] investigated demand response strategies for data centers under coincidence peak pricing schemes by combining workload shifting and local generation. The study developed stochastic and robust optimization algorithms to reduce peak demand and energy costs. However, practical energy management aspects, including tariff-based demand response and operational scheduling of local energy resources, are not proposed a addressed demand-response-based optimal operation framework for data centers based on a distributed multi-energy system that jointly supplies electricity, cooling, and heat. The multi-energy system integrates multiple components such as PV, wind turbines, ground source heat pumps, internal combustion engines, electric and absorption chillers, and energy

storage devices, and integrated demand response strategy that combines time-of-use (TOU) pricing with renewable energy peak subsidies. Wang et al. [9] proposed a proactive demand response framework for geographically distributed data centers based on dynamic electricity pricing. The interactions between data centers and the power grid were formulated as a two-stage bilevel optimization problem to achieve both energy cost reduction and grid load balancing. Yet the study does not examine the operational scheduling of local energy resources. Zhang et al. [10] developed a coordinated optimization framework for multiple geographically distributed data centers interacting with a grid load aggregator to reduce electricity demand while maintaining their individual quality-of-service (QoS) constraints. By contrast, the present study focuses on a single data center and proposes a localized facility-level energy management system (EMS) that jointly coordinates local BESS operation, intra-day computational workload flexibility, physical cooling dynamics, and equipment-ageing-related cost components. Recent studies have also considered integrated workload and energy management in data centers. Guo et al. [11] developed an integrated management framework that coordinates workload transfer and energy system operation by considering the trade-off between operational cost and service-related performance. Rostami et al. [12] focused on thermal-aware workload distribution under demand variations and optimized spatial workload allocation among servers using a model predictive control approach. These studies highlight the importance of coupling computational workload management with energy and thermal behavior. However, they either emphasize service delay and quality-of-service metrics or server-level spatial workload distribution. In contrast, the present study focuses on day-ahead temporal workload flexibility at the facility level and represents heterogeneous computational tasks through three explicit mathematical flexibility classes: short-running deferrable workloads, long-running continuous workloads, and long-running interruptible workloads.

The integration of local energy resources has become another important research direction for data center energy management. Wang et al. [13] provided a review-oriented study on data-center integrated energy systems, including waste heat utilization, multi-energy configurations, and sustainability challenges. While these studies provide valuable insights into multi-energy data center operation, the present work addresses a different problem scale by developing a deterministic

Thermal management and waste heat recovery are also critical aspects of sustainable data center operation. Chen et al. [14] experimentally investigated an integrated cooling and waste heat recovery system capable of switching between different operating modes according to ambient temperature and evaluated its energy-saving performance using ERE and PUE indicators. Hao et al. [15] reviewed data center waste heat recovery technologies, including heating, cooling, and power generation applications, and assessed their performance using an exergy-based evaluation framework. Han et al. [16] examined waste heat reutilization and integrated demand response for geographically distributed data centers interacting with heating networks through a decentralized ADMM-based optimization algorithm. In addition, Cui et al. [17] proposed a waste-heat-driven cooling scheme using a triple-stage absorption chiller to recover low-grade data center thermal discharge, while Ebrahimi et al. [18] evaluated the integration of on-chip two-phase cooling with absorption refrigeration for waste heat recovery. Gonzalez et al. These studies mainly focus on thermodynamic system design, waste heat recovery potential, or district-level heat interaction. By contrast, the present study does not propose a new cooling device; instead, it incorporates the operational and ageing cost effects of the cooling system into a facility-level EMS and coordinates cooling demand with workload scheduling, BESS operation, and PV utilization.

Learning-based, stochastic, and risk-aware approaches have also been explored for data center and energy system optimization. One of the research in the literature presents a decentralized approach for data centers to engage in grid-balancing programs without operational privacy. The primary challenge identified is the information gap, standard cost-minimization models rely on future predictions of power prices and incoming tasks, which are difficult to pinpoint in real-time. By employing a federated reinforcement learning framework, the system enables data centers to learn optimal energy behaviors collaboratively [20]. Another study identifies a strategic framework for achieving carbon neutrality in data centers by integrating policy and technology. It maps the industry's carbon footprint and summarizes the sustainability initiatives of major cloud providers. The authors advocate for three-pillar strategies such as increasing renewable energy, optimizing energy efficiency, and enhancing energy reuse [21]. In another research, authors quantify the environmental toll of data storage by analyzing global electricity generation mixes. It expands the sustainability conservation beyond carbon, incorporating water and land footprints to reveal the holistic impact of data centers. A critical finding is the staggering cost of dark data which is unused or forgotten information. If unmanaged, this digital waste could annually consume 41.65 gigalitres of water and 59.4 square kilometers of land, while emitting 5.26 million tons of CO2 [22]. Khan et al. [23] reviewed the role of machine learning in demand response applications, including real-time decision making, consumer behavior modeling, and secure energy transactions. Pallonetto et al. Utilizing real-world datasets, they highlight the critical role of feature selection and pre-processing in predictive accuracy. Xing et al. [25] identify a significant disconnect between theoretical DR frameworks and industrial implementation. The authors delineate five systemic barriers: excessive architectural complexity, the disruption of established management abstractions, and over-reliance on deferrable batch processing, a dearth of dynamic operational strategies, and insufficient economic incentives. Rectifying these structural and fiscal misalignments is imperative for transitioning toward a sustainable, high efficiency computational ecosystem.

Xu et al. [26] developed a hybrid robust-stochastic optimization framework for a multi-energy microgrid incorporating hydrogen-based conversion and storage with data center demand response. Yuan et al. [27] proposed a distributionally robust day-ahead scheduling framework using a demand response aggregator and a distributed FAST-PP-ADMM method. More recently, Niazi et al. [28] investigated risk-constrained spatio-temporal flexibility by coordinating mobile energy storage systems and data centers under uncertainty in distribution systems. Although these studies provide advanced solutions for uncertainty handling, decentralized decision making, and risk-aware operation, they generally require scenario generation, uncertainty sets, training data, or multi-agent coordination structures. The present study instead develops a transparent and reproducible deterministic day-ahead optimization framework that can serve as a benchmark for future uncertainty-aware or learning-based extensions.

Based on the above review, existing studies have investigated data center demand response, workload shifting, renewable integration, storage-supported operation, waste heat recovery, absorption cooling, and uncertainty-aware optimization from different perspectives. However, limited attention has been given to a single-site data center EMS that jointly coordinates PV generation, BESS operation, heterogeneous intra-day workload flexibility, indoor thermal dynamics, cooling-system ageing cost, and carbon-performance indicators under a unified dayahead optimization framework. To address this gap, the present study proposes a MILP-based EMS for a PV'BESS-supported data center. The model explicitly represents three workload flexibility classes, integrates thermal constraints and cooling operation, accounts for BESS and cooling ageing costs, and evaluates economic and environmental performance using total operating cost, grid interaction, cooling energy, carbon usage effectiveness (CUE), CFE, and avoided emissions.

## Workload flexibility modeling and EMS integration

Demand Response (DR) is a broad operational paradigm where electricity consumers adjust consumption in response to price signals, grid conditions, or system-level optimization objectives. In data centers, DR strategies including geographic load balancing, power capping, local generation dispatch, storage coordination, thermal pre-cooling, and workload shifting offer demand-side flexibility by modifying computational demand temporally without compromising service continuity.

In this study, DR is implemented via workload shifting as the primary flexibility lever (computational load modulation), rather than multiple instruments. The demand response strategy is operationalized through structured workload redistribution, as in the following subsections.

workload modulation and temporal shifting. Prior studies have demonstrated that workload shifting can reduce operational costs, mitigate peak demand, and support grid flexibility [29'32]. Workload flexibility in data centers is typically categorized as delay-tolerant, deferrable, and interruptible workloads [33,34].

These classes are not introduced as standalone algorithmic contributions; rather, they provide structured constraint sets through which heterogeneous workload flexibility is represented and coordinated within the proposed EMS optimization framework.

Short-running deferrable workloads consist of aggregated batches of jobs such as data backups, virus scanning, log cleaning, or non-urgent analytics. These require brief execution times without demanding immediate responses. In the original schedule, they are assigned to specific hours but can be flexibly shifted to neighboring periods within a limited window, as long as total processing completes the same day. Mode B models non-interruptible, long-duration computational workloads that execute as a single, continuous block without interruption or partitioning into smaller sub-tasks, while preserving their original power consumption profile. Typical examples include large-scale batch processing jobs, continuous simulation tasks, and extended AI model training runs. These applications require structural integrity during execution, disallowing partial deferral or fragmentation. Within the EMS optimization, Mode B represents a shape-preserving workload class in which the temporal position of a continuous workload block is selected while preserving its duration and power profile.

Mode C represents globally redistributable computational workloads commonly found in cloud-based, virtualized, and distributed processing environments [31,35]. Unlike rigid or locally constrained models, it captures tasks that can be flexibly scheduled, interrupted, partitioned into sub-tasks, and redistributed across the entire time horizon. Examples include distributed data analytics, cloud-based elastic jobs, MapReduce-type parallel processing, and virtual machine migrations.

In these systems, processes are unbound from fixed temporal structures and can be dynamically reshaped to achieve objectives like cost minimization, peak reduction, or renewable energy alignment. Based on the developed mathematical framework, the data center EMS problem is formulated as a constrained optimization model. The objective is to minimize total operational cost, including grid electricity, battery ageing, and HVAC ageing costs, subject to power balance constraints, operational constraints associated with the representative workload flexibility classes, battery energy dynamics, indoor thermal dynamics, temperature limits, and operational bounds on grid exchange, BESS power/energy, and cooling capacity.

The proposed EMS is formulated as a mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) problem. The objective function and all operational constraints are linear, while binary variables are introduced to enforce mutually exclusive operating modes such as grid import/export and BESS charge/discharge.

Accordingly, the continuous relaxation of the model is a linear programming problem, whereas the complete formulation is a MILP due to the inclusion of binary decision variables. The problem is solved using MATLAB intlinprog, which provides a solver-based optimality certificate through the reported MIP gap.

Based on the coupling among electrical balance, workload shifting, BESS operation, and thermal dynamics, the energy management problem is formulated within the proposed optimization framework. The model is implemented in MATLAB, where the solver iteratively updates decision variables while satisfying all operational constraints to determine the optimal hourly scheduling strategy. As outlined in Fig. 9, the procedure loads system inputs (workloads, PV generation, electricity prices, and battery parameters), computes total IT demand including shifted workloads, applies system constraints, evaluates the objective function, and determines the cost-minimizing operating schedule.

The proposed EMS is evaluated over a 24-hour day-ahead scheduling horizon with hourly time steps. This resolution is consistent with the available workload, PV generation, electricity tariff, and ambient temperature data. Although the formulation can be extended to finer time resolutions or longer horizons, doing so would increase the number of

decision variables and constraints and, consequently, the computational burden. Therefore, the hourly 24-hour horizon is adopted as a practical day-ahead EMS setting in this study.

For reproducibility, the MILP model was implemented in MATLAB using the Optimization Toolbox intlinprog solver. The decision vector includes grid import/export power, BESS charge/discharge power, BESS energy, cooling power, Mode-A allocation variables, Mode-C reshaped workload variables, binary variables for grid and BESS operating modes, Mode-B binary start variables, and minute-level indoor temperature states. Equality constraints are assembled in Aeq and beq matrices, including power balance, BESS energy dynamics, workload conservation, terminal SOC, and thermal dynamics, while inequality constraints are assembled in Aineq and bineq matrices for peak demand limits, binary exclusivity constraints, and indoor temperature bounds. The lower and upper bounds of all variables are explicitly defined before calling intlinprog. The main input data consists of 24-hour workload profiles, ambient temperature, solar irradiance, electricity tariff, PV parameters, BESS parameters, and thermal parameters, as summarized in Table 3 and the corresponding model equations.

For industrial implementation, the same optimization structure can be deployed as a supervisory EMS layer that receives updated measurements and forecasts from existing BEMS/DCIM platforms, PV inverters, BESS controllers, grid meters, and cloud workload schedulers. The optimized outputs can then be translated into set-points for workload scheduling, BESS charge/discharge operation, grid exchange, and cooling system operation.

## Conclusion

This study developed an integrated energy management framework for a PV'BESS-supported data center in which representative heterogeneous workload flexibility classes are coordinated with BESS operation, PV utilization, grid exchange, and thermal constraints under timeof-use tariffs. The proposed model minimizes grid electricity cost, BESS degradation cost, and cooling system ageing cost while evaluating carbon performance using T'rkiye-specific grid parameters.

The results show that workload shifting is the dominant flexibility mechanism for both economic and environmental improvement. Across all workload modes, shifting IT demand toward PV-rich midday periods and low-tariff nighttime hours significantly reduces peak electricity costs. In the combined Mode (A+B+C) case, daily operating cost decreases from 658 USD in the base scenario to 528 USD with shifting alone. This reduction is also supported by lower cooling ageing costs, as redistributed loads mitigate evening thermal accumulation and enable operation closer to the upper thermal constraint.

BESS integration provides additional flexibility by temporally decoupling PV generation from demand. The BESS charges during PV surplus or low-price periods and discharges during high-tariff hours, reducing peak grid purchases. While the standalone economic impact of BESS is smaller than that of workload shifting, storage enhances system coordination. In the combined Mode (A+B+C) scenario, BESS alone reduces cost from 658 USD to 623 USD, while the joint BESS + shifting strategy achieves the lowest cost of 492 USD, demonstrating the synergistic benefits of combining computational flexibility with storage.

Carbon performance improvements are also substantial. Under T'rkiye -specific parameters, the integrated BESS + shifting configuration reduces average CUE from 0.455 to 0.349 kgCO?/kWhIT. At the same time, the CFE score increases from 66.2% to 72.7%, indicating greater utilization of carbon-free electricity. Total avoided emissions rise from 2574 kgCO? to 3325 kgCO?, confirming that coordinated renewable integration and flexibility deployment significantly enhance emission mitigation.
