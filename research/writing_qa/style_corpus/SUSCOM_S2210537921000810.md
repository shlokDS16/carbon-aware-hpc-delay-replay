## Abstract

The rise in the penetration of the internet across the world has led to a rapid increase in the consumption of energy at the data centers established by leading cloud data service providers. High power consumption by these data centers [DCs] leads to high operational costs and high carbon emissions into the environment. From a sustainability point of view, the ultimate goal is to maximize the productivity and efficiency of these data centers while keeping greenhouse gas emissions to the minimum and maximize data center productivity. This goal can be achieved by better resource utilization and replacing carbon-intensive approaches of energy production with green sources of energy. Due to the limited intermittent availability of renewable sources of energy, the ideal 'Green' design for the DCs, should incorporate inter-operability with both renewable and non-renewable sources of energy. In this paper, we propose a ren-aware scheduler to schedule computational workload by prioritizing their execution within the duration of green energy availability on the basis of the predicted hourly green energy and workload data of DCs. Our results demonstrate that our ren-aware scheduler can increase the green energy consumption by 51% compared to the conventional randomized scheduler that distributes load without considering green energy and load. It can also reduce the total energy consumption by 25% by putting the DCs to sleep during their idle time, as it saves 4.5 times more idle energy than the randomized scheduler. Additionally, the results also demonstrate how the role of time zones of the DCs and the duration of green energy availability in them is pivotal in our ren-aware scheduler's performance.

## System model and ren-aware scheduler

Our system model consists of geographically dispersed DCs. We consider solar energy as the green source of energy available to them. In addition to captive solar panels generating solar energy (green energy), DCs also have a constant supply of non-renewable energy (brown energy). The DCs store hourly solar irradiance data for the prediction of solar energy. They also store the CPU load data for each minute. They transfer this data to the prediction module responsible for predicting the magnitude of the solar irradiance at a DC at every hour, and the load at every minute, on a particular day. Each DC has a broker associated with it that updates its information about the green energy and load capacity on an hourly basis and forwards it to the ren-aware scheduler. The jobs obtained from various cloud computing sources in a particular hour are forwarded to this scheduler. The ren-aware scheduler is responsible for allocating jobs to the DCs based on each DC's priority in green energy and capacity to carry out a compute job. Information about this allocation is transferred to the broker and based on this, jobs are scheduled to different DCs (Fig. 1).

The two major components of our system model are the prediction module and the ren-aware scheduler. We use CloudSim for the simulation of this model.

Our design being modular, it is possible for different algorithms (e.g., using machine learning) to be applied in place of the ones we have used for illustration.

Scheduling of computing loads such that there is an opportunistic advantage of green energy availability is possible only if the job scheduler is aware of the likely availability before distributing the job. If the scheduler has prior information about how much green energy would be available at each of the DCs and how much loads they would be carrying at a particular time, it can distribute the load on them optimally. The prediction module is responsible for predicting this hourly green energy and load at the DCs. Based on these predicted values, the scheduler prioritizes the DCs and allocates them jobs. The prediction module updates the broker each hour with predicted solar irradiance data in that hour and current load each minute of that hour. We have used the ARIMA model for forecasting generated renewable energy and load forecasting for all datacenters. ARIMA(autoregressive integrated moving average) model, is used generally for understanding and predicting the future points in a series. In paper [2], Alduailij tested different energy forecasting scenarios using predictive models, including Regression, ARIMA time series, exponential smoothing time series, artificial and deep neural network. ARIMA model had outperformed the other approaches with an accuracy of 98.91%. Even while conducting our experiments, simulations were run on both ARIMA-predicted weather data and the original historical weather data. The results obtained on the two datasets were similar.

We use the ARIMA(7,1,0) [4] model to predict the availability of green energy at the DCs. It takes the hourly solar irradiance data of the previous 49 days to predict the hourly solar irradiance during a day at a DC. It takes an area of the solar farm and efficiency of the solar panels in the solar farm of the DCs to calculate the solar energy generated.

We use the ARIMA(5,1,0) model to predict the load that would be present at DCs. Based on this predicted load, it is decided if a DC would be able to take up more load by comparing it with the maximum load capacity of DC which is ? 2.2.1 It takes the load data of each minute in the previous 3 days to predict the load for each minute of the day.

The ren-aware scheduler is responsible for allocating jobs to the geographically distributed DCs for each minute during a day, so that the overall green energy out of the total energy consumed by all the DCs in a day, is maximum. It computes the job's start time and selects the DC for its execution, with an aim to increase the green energy consumption over brown energy for job execution. This is done by allocating a job to a DC that has higher green energy available than others, while also having the threshold load capacity to complete the job. If no green DC is available within the waiting time of a job, then it gets scheduled on a non-green DC.

## Results

To evaluate the performance of the ren-aware scheduler towards increasing the green energy consumption and the overall efficiency of the DCs, we compare it with the conventional randomized scheduler. In the latter, load is distributed among the DCs randomly, without considering the availability of green energy and load capacity at the DCs [15]. We refer to this approach as randomized and ours as ren-aware. It is to be noted that the work of the ren-aware scheduler cannot be directly taken on by a standard scheduling algorithm such as first-fit, best-fit, etc. This is because such well-known heuristics consider only a single parameter (such as the size of the job, which is then matched for 'fit'), which is inadequate in the present context. Such an algorithm yields complete unacceptable results given that we need to take into account several unrelated variables such as sunlight availability, load capacity of a DC, different time zones of various DCs, job deadlines for jobs, etc. It is well known that randomized or stochastic scheduling does much better than such heuristic approaches, under conditions where multiple parameters are involved [50,3].

Therefore, DCs in different time zones give better results for renpercentage, with a trade-off in waiting time for jobs. This suggests that given a use case where a high priority is to increase the renpercentage, while being able to compromise on greater waiting time of jobs, DCs can be selected from different time zones.

In our experiments, the data set we have used for DCs in different time zones have a time difference of nearly 1 h among them. According to this time difference, the improvement we see in Ren-percentage for DCs in different time zones as compared to DCs in the same time zone, is almost 3 percent. This difference can increase further if the time zone difference among them increases, as they become more geographically distant and hence will have access to green energy sources, round the clock. In our experiments, there are jobs with considerable amounts of allowed waiting time. In DCs within the same time zone, jobs may wait for a long time for the green energy sources to become available at a DC, and hence get an opportunity to improve the overall Ren-percentage. For instance, if a job needs to be scheduled at a DC at a time when no sunlight is available (but is about to get sunlight in some time) at any of the DCs, then the job waits to get allocated to DC when sunlight is available (if that time is within the allowed waiting time of the job). On the other hand, jobs in different time zones DCs, have good Renpercentage without consuming much of the allowed waiting time. This happens because DCs are geographically distant, and hence jobs have better possibility to get a DC with sunlight, without waiting, as DCs have different time zones. Hence, as the difference in the time zones of the DCs increases and allowed waiting time for jobs decreases, Renpercentage should possibly improve even further in case of geographically distributed DCs.

## Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a ren-aware scheduler, which schedules jobs on data centers powered by green as well as brown energy. It also tries to increase the overall green energy consumption while meeting the jobs' deadlines. Results demonstrate that our ren-aware scheduler's performs better than the Random scheduler in terms of ren-percentage and overall energy consumption with a trade-off in waiting time for jobs. The data centers are prioritized based on renewable energy availability and load capacity while scheduling jobs, which increases the possibility of green energy consumption. DCs get more idle time, allowing machines in them to be switched off to save on energy consumption. Based on the results, we conclude that the ren-aware scheduler has the potential to bring a significant improvement in renpercentage in situations when the duration of green energy availability is limited.
