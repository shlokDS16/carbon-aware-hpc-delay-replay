## Abstract

The exponential growth of cloud computing has made data center energy consumption a critical environmental challenge, currently accounting for nearly 2% of global electricity use. Despite this, existing cloud infrastructures rely on static, carbon-agnostic scheduling that fails to consider the significant temporal and spatial variability of grid carbon intensity. This paper proposes and evaluates the Context-Aware and Adaptive Resource Allocation (CARA) architecture, a system designed to decouple compute growth from environmental impact. The methodology involves an event-driven architecture that integrates real-time Marginal Emissions Rate (MER) forecasts into a Multi-Objective Optimization (MOO) engine to perform joint spatio-temporal shifting of asynchronous workloads. To evaluate this framework, a prototype was developed using Python 3.9, the ElectricityMap API for real-time grid data, and an emulated cloud execution model. The empirical results demonstrate that CARA achieves a 75.7% reduction in carbon footprint compared to carbon-agnostic baseline schedulers. This significant reduction was achieved under a 12-hour MaxLatency constraints for asynchronous workloads, specifically by exploiting the carbon intensity arbitrage between fossil-fuel-intensive local grids and globally optimal regions during peak renewable penetration windows. Furthermore, the system maintains a negligible scheduling overhead of just 0.54 s and validates a Spatio-Temporal Efficiency Ratio that quantifies the environmental return on latency. These findings confirm that carbon-aware scheduling is a high-impact, low-cost architectural principle that can effectively mitigate the environmental burden of digital infrastructure.

The resulting greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions from this infrastructure have become a focal point for corporate sustainability reporting. Addressing this energy consumption is vital for global climate-mitigation goals, as the regional concentration of compute power imposes immense pressure on local grids and increases avoidable carbon footprint of digital services.

The fundamental problem addressed in this research is that existing cloud orchestration frameworks treat computational resources as geographically and temporally static, leading to significant avoidable emissions because they cannot simultaneously exploit spatial and temporal arbitrage based on marginal grid signals. Consequently, the primary research objective is to develop and evaluate a modular, automated architecture that leverages real-time MER forecasts to perform joint spatio-temporal optimization, thereby minimizing a task's carbon footprint while strictly adhering to application-specific latency constraints.

## Related works

The development of carbon-aware computing has seen significant progress through various industry and academic solutions. One prominent approach is Carbon-Intelligent Compute Management (CICS), which pioneered the use of Virtual Capacity Curves (VCCs) to shape flexible demand based on day-ahead carbon intensity forecasts and cluster-level demand predictions. This system demonstrates that temporal load shifting at a fleet scale is highly practical for asynchronous workloads such as batch data processing and ML training. By specifying the maximum CPU capacity available for flexible tasks during highintensity hours, Google's framework reduces cluster utilization during peaks without violating daily completion constraints [11]. However, a significant gap remains in its accessibility; as a proprietary internal tool, it does not expose explicit per-task latency or carbon footprint budgets to external developers. Furthermore, its optimization logic is tightly coupled with proprietary cluster controllers, suggesting a need for a more portable, vendor-agnostic orchestration layer.

Another significant solution is Carbon Aware SDK (CA-SDK), which provides a developer-facing, pragmatic approach to location and time shifting for long-running high-performance computing (HPC) simulations. By consuming real-time grid signals from providers like Electricity Maps and WattTime, the CA-SDK enables job owners to select optimal execution windows across various cloud regions [10]. While this provides granular control, the burden of ensuring Quality of Service (QoS) and managing the cost-emissions trade-off falls entirely on the application owner. This manual intervention highlights the absence of a policy-driven engine capable of autonomous decision-making.

Beyond purely carbon-centric goals, the financial implications of green computing are critical for enterprise adoption. Hanafy et al. [5] introduced frameworks for optimizing the "cost of green," demonstrating that carbon footprint reductions can be achieved with minimal financial penalties through strategic resource provisioning. Furthermore, the complexity of modern batch processing requires a deeper understanding of task dependencies. Lechowicz et al. [8] proposed precedence-aware scheduling for data processing clusters, arguing that ignoring task order in a carbon-aware context can lead to SLA violations. CARA builds upon these insights by utilizing a Multi-Objective Optimization (MOO) engine that balances emissions savings with both cost and deadline constraints.

A third comparable framework is CarbonFlex, which treats carbonaware resource management as a joint capacity-provisioning and jobscheduling problem. CarbonFlex utilizes elastic scaling at both the cluster and job levels to align compute demand with low-intensity grid periods while strictly respecting queue-level maximum delay parameters. In evaluations using real cluster traces on AWS, CarbonFlex reduced operational emissions by more than 57.5% relative to carbonagnostic baselines [6]. Despite these gains, the framework is primarily limited to temporal shifting within a single cluster and lacks a comprehensive model for joint spatio-temporal optimization across global regions. A critical architectural decision in CARA is the prioritization of Marginal Emissions Rate (MER) as the governing metric for workload orchestration over Average Carbon Intensity (ACI) or Lifecycle Emissions. While ACI is suitable for historical carbon accounting, it fails to reflect the true environmental impact of scheduling decisions. ACI tracks the mean emissions of all active generators, whereas MER identifies the specific emissions of the marginal power plant, typically a fossil-fuelbased peaking unit, that must ramp up or down to meet incremental changes in demand. Consequently, optimizing for MER allows CARA to achieve emissions reduction by displacing the most carbon-intensive units currently online. Furthermore, while Lifecycle Emissions provide a holistic view of embodied carbon, they are largely static and lack the temporal granularity necessary for real-time scheduling. By focusing on the dynamic MER signal, CARA ensures that operational shifts yield the maximum possible reduction in grid-level CO? output.

The Execution Layer translates logical policies into physical infrastructure actions via a Scheduler-Worker Pattern. This design ensures the system remains stateless and cost-efficient, while supporting various orchestration backends, such as Kubernetes CronJobs or Terraform, by simply swapping the cloud-specific SDK calls without modifying the core optimization logic.

To address trust and auditability gaps, CARA integrates components that ensure the integrity of the scheduling decision before it reaches the cloud provider. The output of the orchestrator is an Execution Manifest, which acts as an immutable record of the scheduling decision. Each manifest is cryptographically signed using SHA(cid:0) 256 and contains the target region, scheduled start time, and policy version. This provides a non-repudiation audit trail, allowing organizations to prove the authenticity and integrity of their environmental claims for regulatory or corporate social responsibility reporting. The Infrastructure Controller Interface parses the signed manifest and translates it into cloud-specific SDK calls. By isolating cloud-specific resource provisioning from the main orchestrator, CARA achieves platform agnosticism.

The empirical results, derived from a 3-hour model-training workload simulation across four global AWS regions, validate the feasibility of decoupling compute demand from grid carbon intensity.

The core achievement of the CARA system is a 75.7% reduction in the carbon footprint of asynchronous tasks through automated grid arbitrage. To provide a rigorous comparative framework, the results were benchmarked against a Local-Immediate Baseline. This baseline represents a carbon-agnostic scheduling policy where workloads are dispatched immediately in the local region (ap-southeast(cid:0) 5) without considering grid carbon intensity or temporal flexibility.

## Conclusion and future works

This study demonstrates that the Context-Aware and Adaptive Resource Allocation (CARA) architecture successfully decouples compute demand from grid carbon intensity by treating operational slack in asynchronous, delay-tolerant workloads as a flexible environmental asset. Beyond the empirical achievement of a 75.7% reduction in carbon footprint, the research synthesizes a critical shift in cloud orchestration: the transition from "follow-the-data" to "follow-the-renewables" paradigm. By validating the synergistic relationship between spatial and temporal shifting, this work proves that regional grid selection defines the "efficiency floor," while sub-hour temporal alignment captures the "efficiency ceiling." This dual-layer optimization provides a scalable, end-to-end blueprint for standardizing sustainability within the global cloud control plane without compromising Service Level Agreements (SLAs). The empirical findings are reinforced by an algorithmic sensitivity analysis, showing a consistent mean carbon reduction of 80.3% with a narrow variance (? = 3.9), statistically validating the architecture's reliability for general-purpose cloud workloads.

However, the geographic shifting of workloads introduces complex ethical considerations regarding energy justice, the principle of ensuring safe, affordable, and sustainable energy for all. While CARA optimizes for global carbon abatement, the large-scale migration of compute tasks could inadvertently place stress on local energy infrastructures in

regions with less robust grids. Future deployments must therefore consider "grid citizenship," ensuring that the pursuit of lower emissions does not destabilize regional electricity markets or displace local energy needs in developing economies.

The path toward production-grade sustainability involves transitioning from emulated simulations to native container orchestration. Future iterations of CARA will focus on developing a custom Kubernetes Operator designed to automate cross-region pod migration based on real-time MER signals and the Spatio-Temporal Efficiency Ratio. This implementation will utilize a confidence-aware MOO engine that incorporates machine learning-based probabilistic intervals to mitigate the risks of forecast volatility. By integrating cryptographic manifest verification directly into the Kubernetes control plane, the framework will provide an immutable and auditable trail of environmental impact, bridging the gap between algorithmic optimization and verifiable corporate ESG reporting.
