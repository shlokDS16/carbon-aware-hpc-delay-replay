## Abstract

## Introduction

The deployment of Large Language Models (LLMs) in multi-Graphics Processing Unit (GPU) environments faces significant challenges regarding energy consumption and load distribution. While most research focuses on optimizing inference throughput, there is a critical lack of frameworks bridging fine-grained telemetry with proactive, energy-aware load balancing. This paper presents a modular forecasting driven perception layer that leverages near real-time GPU power telemetry to enable optimized workload allocation. Using fine-grained telemetry from an operational High Performance Computing (HPC) cluster, we evaluate stateof-the-art time-series architectures, including Spiking Neural Networks (SNN), Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN), Transformers, and Structured State Space Models (SSSM). These models are assessed across operational horizons of 30 s for near-instantaneous balancing and 1 min for near-future system stability. Our results demonstrate that the Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) achieves superior performance, with a Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of 7.97 W for the 30 s window and 9.7 W for the 1 min window. By establishing a validated forecasting backbone, this approach provides a plug-and-play forecasting component that can be integrated into Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) or heuristic schedulers, offering a scalable solution to improve the sustainability and efficiency of large-scale LLM serving.

The utilization of Large Language Models (LLMs) has grown exponentially since OpenAI unveiled its ChatGPT 3.5 model in November 2022, introducing a freely accessible conversational AI tool designed on the concept of reinforcement learning from human feedback [1]. Thereafter, numerous new LLMs have emerged, but they all share a common limitation: they prioritize answer performance over energy consumption, assuming infinite resources. Typically, the deployment of LLMs consists of an architecture comprising multiple powerful Graphics Processing Units (GPUs). As highlighted in [2], one of the main challenges lies in distributing tasks across these multiple GPUs to achieve optimal parallelization. While traditional High Performance Computing (HPC) energy management focuses on batch job scheduling, there is a specific research gap in energy-aware allocation for the realtime, bursty nature of LLM serving. With the rapid expansion of Large Language Models (LLMs), the awareness of the scientific community regarding their energy consumption has increased significantly. Since the adoption of this technology has grown exponentially, its energy footprint has followed suit. Nevertheless, technology-specific metrics and energy mitigation techniques have not kept pace, resulting in a critical gap between deployment and efficiency. A notable example of this issue is the emissions associated with the training of the BLOOM model, a 176billion parameter LLM that emitted approximately 50.5 tons of CO2eq during its development and training [4]. It is essential to highlight that the carbon footprint of LLM serving is generally categorized into two segments: embodied emissions, which pertain to the manufacturing, deployment, and disposal of hardware; and operational emissions, which represent the carbon footprint associated with training, inference, and maintenance [5].

The shift toward Green AI has prompted the development of specialized benchmarks for LLM efficiency, such as energy-per-query [6]. In this context, tools like CodeCarbon provide first-order estimates of CO2 emissions by tracking CPU, GPU, and memory consumption alongside location-dependent carbon intensity. While CodeCarbon is an excellent tool for comparative analysis and auditing the energy footprint of AI models, it typically relies on software-based power estimates and non-continuous tracking. Consequently, it lacks the highfrequency telemetry and continuous integration required for real-time orchestration in LLM serving, where GPU power fluctuations must be monitored at an operational scale to drive proactive load-balancing decisions.

However, this perspective only provides a partial view of the issue. As noted in [7], the comprehensive carbon footprint of LLMs extends to several often-overlooked aspects, including hardware lifecycle, the collection of massive datasets, and training. Due to the high proportion of operational expenses attributed to inference ' with estimates suggesting it accounts for up to 90% of the overall costs [8] ' most research efforts have focused on optimizing inference efficiency. This is further supported by recent surveys on LLM sustainability, which highlight that as these models transition from research to large-scale commercial

applications, their operational energy demand in data centers begins to rival their massive training requirements [9]. In this context, a recent large-scale benchmarking framework [10] has quantified the environmental footprint across dozens of state-of-the-art models, revealing that gains in per-query efficiency are often offset by the massive expansion in total usage, which is a phenomenon known as the Jevons Paradox. These findings emphasize that sustainability in AI deployment depends not only on model architecture but also on the underlying infrastructure-aware metrics that can account for real-world datacenter conditions. However, a significant challenge is that measuring and predicting the energy consumption of LLM inference is difficult. This difficulty arises because the choice of hardware platform substantially impacts the power consumption of inference tasks [11]. In this sense, recent benchmarking efforts have begun to characterize the energy profile of state-of-the-art models and multi-node configurations [12]. These studies reveal that inference energy costs, while often overshadowed by training, represent a critical scaling bottleneck. Furthermore, the heterogeneity of modern platforms, comprising a multitude of GPU and CPU types, often makes it challenging to extrapolate results. Additionally, one of the primary problems is that LLMs generate output token by token, resulting in unpredictable query lengths that require multiple iterations of the model for each batch. Moreover, LLM-based services require large memory capacity and high memory bandwidth, given that these models typically have tens to hundreds of billions of parameters [13], complicating the development of energy-aware LLM systems.

To mitigate these challenges, the authors in [14] propose a predictive scheduling system for data center resources, which uses the source code as input and predicts the execution time and GPU energy consumption as output. While these static analysis methods provide a baseline, they often fail to capture the dynamic, non-linear fluctuations of power consumption during live LLM inference. Some works use GPU Dynamic Voltage Frequency Scaling (DVFS) to reduce energy consumption, leveraging the approximately linear relationship between the clock frequency of the GPU core and its supply voltage [15].

The energy consumption of a GPU is modeled by multiplying the GPU chip architecture energy efficiency, the number of FLOPs per local dataset divided by the number of FLOPs per cycle, and the square of the clock frequency [16], as described in Eq. (1). In [17], DVFS is used to adjust the GPU frequency during a query, at the granularity of a few iterations, with the goal of reducing energy consumption. To determine the optimal frequency given a specific query arrival rate (RPS) that meets a predefined Service-Level Objective (SLO), a model is developed that forecasts the tail latency for different frequencies.

An alternative approach to reducing energy consumption is workload shifting to data centers or regions with lower carbon intensity [8]. However, this strategy is only feasible in specific scenarios. Other researchers have attempted to execute LLMs locally on mobile devices to mitigate communication overhead with servers and provide more secure environments.

Nevertheless, according to [18], current LLM implementations face significant challenges in providing consistent outputs on mobile devices due to constrained architectural design spaces. Only certain models with 2B parameters can provide full functionality, albeit at the cost of substantially higher latency ' exceeding 30 seconds ' compared to cloud-based deployments, which typically respond in under 10 s. In [12], it is concluded that incremental increases in inference energy per second are not always technically justified, as they may only yield marginal improvements in throughput.

This section details the data collection and preprocessing methodologies fundamental to the development of the Energy-aware Intelligent Load Balancing System. The primary is to construct high-quality timeseries datasets that feed the forecasting module'the system's perception layer'which in turn provides accurate predictions to inform the load balancing logic for LLM serving.

To ensure the dataset is representative of diverse operational states, telemetry data was acquired within the KATEA HPC environment, where four NVIDIA H100 nodes were continuously monitored over a four-month period while serving various language models to internal institutional users. This extensive collection period captures a wide range of workload patterns, from idle states to peak-demand inference bursts, providing a robust foundation for the training and validation of the predictive architectures. The workload characteristic reflects the normal usage of an enterprise LLM. The telemetry data was collected at a sampling interval of 30 s, resulting in 344,925 total samples. For the 1-minute time window, an average of both 30-second values was calculated, yielding 202,179 samples. This data includes detailed metrics on GPU utilization and energy consumption, which are summarized in Table 4 and serve as the foundation for training and evaluating the predictive models.

The collected data was preprocessed in an adequate data pipeline, involving data normalization and feature selection. An overview of the preprocessing pipeline proposed in this paper can be found in Fig. 2.

This study adopts multivariate time series forecasting to address the inherent complexity of GPU power dynamics. Multivariate approaches often leverage a combination of heterogeneous AI solutions; for instance, [27] demonstrates a hybrid approach combining Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN), LSTM, and Random Forest (RF) for energy-related predictions.

To ensure optimal performance, systematic data preprocessing is paramount. Data curation is a pivotal step in the development of accurate and reliable models [28], as the fidelity of the input data is inextricably linked with model performance. Low-quality or noisy telemetry can yield inaccurate, biased, or misleading forecasts, regardless of the underlying model architecture.

Specifically, we implement data normalization and the redundancy of highly correlated features via a Pearson correlation filter. Data normalization is a critical procedure that involves scaling numeric data to a common range ' typically [0, 1] ' to prevent features with larger magnitudes from disproportionately influencing the loss function. This ensures all telemetry variables are treated equally, enabling the model to learn underlying temporal dependencies more efficiently. In this study, Min'Max normalization was chosen to linearly transform the data (Eq. (2)) because it preserves relative power variations and maintains bounded ranges, which is consistent with established practices in prior GPU power modeling studies.

On the other hand, feature selection is a vital component of machine learning pipelines, as it can significantly impact model performance. It consists of identifying a subset of the most informative features from the original dataset, eliminating redundant or irrelevant features that can negatively impact model performance. By selecting the most informative features, models can learn more effectively and provide better results. One common approach to feature selection is using correlation analysis to identify and eliminate highly correlated features. In this case, Pearson correlation is going to be used, as it is a widely used method for measuring the linear relationship between two variables.

This methodology consists of excluding features with a correlation greater than 90%. This threshold is employed as a standard technique to reduce multicollinearity while acknowledging the potential loss of some causal signals, only retaining the most informative features for the final model.

Using the preprocessed data, several AI models were trained, validated and tested. The dataset was partitioned by reserving 10% of the total data for testing, while 15% of the remaining training data was allocated for validation to identify the best-performing model for predicting the power usage of each GPU. To maintain data continuity, missing values were handled by carrying over the previous available value, while outliers were not filtered to ensure the models remain robust to the peak fluctuations typical of HPC workloads. Furthermore, the reliability of the DCGM-based power measurements used in this study is supported by prior validation studies. After applying a correlation filter, the selected features included node frequency metrics, GPU memory and utilization metrics, and interconnect/bus data. Temporal context was provided by hour, minute, and a one-hot encoding for the day of the week, alongside system-level metrics including I/O time, disk read/write operations, and memory states (buffer, cached, free, and total bytes). This selection process yielded a total of 48 features. Two scenarios have been considered: predicting the power usage for the next 30 s to balance current requests, and predicting the power usage for the next minute to anticipate and prepare for future requests. Overfitting risks are mitigated via cross-validation, dropout, and evaluation on unseen temporal segments. Due to its long-term memory capability, LSTMs are frequently regarded as among the fundamental deep-learning algorithms for time series [29]. This type of network was designed to capture long-term dependencies in data, a critical requirement for forecasting in domains such as hydrological [30], electricity power generation [31], or water quality [32] prediction. These RNN variants overcome the vanishing-exploding gradient problem by employing gating mechanisms to control non-linear inputs and outputs of the cell. This enables LSTMs to retain valuable information early in the sequence, skip irrelevant observations, and reset the state as needed [33]. The LSTM model contains three primary gates: the input gate, forget gate, and output gate. At each time step, the LSTM cell receives the input data, the previous hidden state, and the cell state, controls the flow and storage of information based on the gating mechanism.

' The input gate determines the information that should be updated in the cell state, generating a value between 0 and 1 to indicate the importance of each piece of information.

' The forget gate decides which information should be discarded from the cell state, generating a value between 0 and 1 to represent the amount of information retained and control the impact of past information on the current state.

' The output gate is responsible for determining the hidden state as well as the output value at the current time step, generating a control value between 0 and 1.

LSTM can be combined with other artificial intelligence solutions, as shown in [34], where a hybrid approach combines LSTM and genetic algorithms to optimize time window size and topology for predicting the Korea Stock Market Price Index. An extension of the LSTM is the Bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM), which enhances learning capability by

processing data in both forward and backward directions. Unlike a regular LSTM layer, which only captures previous context, the BiLSTM aggregates subsequent context by adding a second LSTM layer that moves in the reverse direction. By combining the outputs of both directions, the BiLSTM provides a more comprehensive representation. For example, the BiLSTM has been used to predict stock prices with heavytailed time series [35] and to predict full-scale ship manoeuvering motions at sea under random rudder actions [36]. These examples demonstrate the effectiveness of the BiLSTM in capturing complex patterns in temporal data.

The fundamental difference between SNNs and ANNs is that the former process discrete events rather than continuous data. This enables an event-driven mechanism where neurons only fire when necessary. The limitations of Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Transformers in handling long-range dependencies have led to the development of State Space Models (SSMs), specifically the Structured State Space Sequence model (S4). S4 offers a solution to capture longsequence dependencies while significantly reducing memory overhead. This is achieved through two key mechanisms: parallel computation over long sequences and the efficient representation of past information using the High-Order Polynomial Projection Operator (HiPPO). HiPPO enables the model to control the retention or decay of information through an optimal polynomial projection mechanism [49], effectively addressing the memory limitations of traditional RNNs and Transformers.

Compared to Transformers, which exhibit quadratic complexity with respect to sequence length, the S4 model achieves linear complexity due to its utilization of the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) for parallelizable training.

To enhance the efficiency and sustainability of LLM serving operations, our system proposes the integration of energy consumption forecasting into the load balancing process. This approach builds upon the work of Janumpally et al. [26], which explores intelligent load balancing strategies in distributed systems. While their work focuses on general-purpose optimization, our contribution adapts these principles to the specific requirements of LLM serving within HPC environments. In our conceptual framework, the load balancing module is designed to receive real-time predictions of the energy consumption associated with incoming LLM requests. These predictions are generated by the forecasting subsystem described in Section 5.3, which utilizes telemetry data from the KATEA HPC infrastructure. Unlike reactive systems, our perception layer provides the situational awareness necessary to transition from simple task distribution to proactive resource orchestration. For instance, in high-frequency environments (30s/1min), any scheduling logic is entirely dependent on the reliability of the underlying telemetry and forecasts to ensure future system stability.

This integration is intrinsically linked to the stability of Service Level Agreements (SLAs). Drawing parallels from heterogeneous streaming frameworks like Apache Flink, energy saving scheduling must integrally consider node response time and throughput to avoid performance degradation [50]. In energy-constrained workflows, the tradeoff between minimal schedule time and energy consumption is a zero-sum game where predictive foresight is the only way to optimize both variables simultaneously [51].

By providing a validated 30s/1min forecasting window, our framework is intended to serve as the critical state-input that advanced schedulers need. For example, hybrid algorithms such as Battle Royale Deep Reinforcement Learning (BRDRL) [52] require high-fidelity state representation to optimize job transfers and VM selection. Our system provides a sufficiently tight bound to allow such DRL agents or bio-inspired schedulers [53] to perform proactive frequency scaling.

The optimization of load balancing and resource orchestration remains an ongoing area of research. Future work will focus on refining the forecasting models, expanding the scope of telemetry features, and exploring reinforcement learning-based strategies to further improve system adaptability and energy efficiency.

To validate the practical viability of this selection in a real-world, continuous operation, we deployed the dual-service GRU monitor (covering both 30 s and 1 min horizons) on a Raspberry Pi 4 (Cortex-A72) for a 24-hour stress test. The system performed high-frequency inference every 100 ms, successfully simulating the sub-second decision loop required for live LLM load balancing. Monitoring the execution with CodeCarbon, the service consumed a total of only 0.228 kWh over the entire period, with an average CPU power draw of 7.59 W and a highly efficient CPU utilization of 6.65%. Crucially, the empirical performance metrics confirmed the real-time suitability of the models: the average inference latency was 47.28 ms for the 30 s horizon and 46.29 ms for the 1 min horizon, with a combined P95 tail latency of 239 ms. Furthermore, the system maintained a minimal memory footprint, peaking at only 358.88 MB of RAM. This negligible overhead demonstrates the technical viability of the GRU-based forecasting layer to provide critical predictive intelligence while remaining lightweight enough to be integrated into HPC management stacks without becoming a latency bottleneck or an energy burden.

Notably, the 30-second window uses double the data compared to the 1-minute window, resulting in significantly higher training time and energy consumption. It is worth noting that the increased data volume in the 30-second window does not necessarily yield better results than the 1-minute window. In terms of MAE, the 30-second window shows slightly better performance (almost 2 W lower error), but the 1-minute window outperforms it in RMSE. This discrepancy arises because the 30-second window exhibits greater variability in data patterns, making predictions more challenging. While the larger dataset improves MAE (due to better overall trends), the increased variability leads to worse RMSE and ?2 metrics, as errors are amplified by higher noise levels.

The performance of the SNN model was inferior to the other architectures, as it was unable to capture the temporal dependencies as effectively as the RNN-based models. Nevertheless, its theoretical energy efficiency was notable, as SNN neurons are only active when a spike occurs, resulting in lower instantaneous energy usage. This makes the SNN a promising candidate for energy-constrained systems where power minimization is prioritized over high-precision forecasting.

However, in this specific case, the LSTM model outperformed the SNN in terms of energy consumption, MAE, and RMSE, achieving results comparable to the other complex models. Since the LSTM model demonstrated the lowest total energy consumption while maintaining comparable MAE and RMSE in both forecasting horizons, it represents the most suitable choice for systems with low energy requirements that still demand reliable predictive accuracy.

Furthermore, it is important to acknowledge the inherent trade-off between energy efficiency and inference latency in LLM serving. While the ultimate objective of an integrated load balancer is to optimize both dimensions, a detailed empirical analysis of latency is outside the scope of this study. This is due to the fact that latency is highly dependent on specific hardware interconnects, network topologies, and management strategies of different serving frameworks. Consequently, this research focuses on the critical prerequisite: establishing a high-fidelity energy forecasting layer that intended to provide the necessary decision support for managing these trade-offs in various HPC environments.

These findings support the feasibility of integrating forecasting into the load balancing system and highlight the importance of model selection based on the temporal resolution of the forecasting task.

This paper provides a comprehensive overview of the energy efficiency metrics relevant to LLM serving systems, as well as techniques employed to mitigate energy consumption. Furthermore, it offers an in-depth examination of the most effective current AI techniques for time series forecasting, allowing for a nuanced understanding of each technique's strengths and weaknesses, as well as its associated energy consumption. This study has provided a detailed analysis of all the particularities of LLM serving systems, along with an architecture for metric collection and a model stack for predicting GPU power usage, thereby offering critical foundational information that could support system load balancing. Specifically, our architecture serves as a ''perception layer'' within the broader ecosystem of autonomous resource management. By providing high-fidelity, short-horizon energy forecasts, this work acts as a modular, ''plug-and-play'' component that can be integrated into heuristic or Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) schedulers.

To evaluate the practical implications of this ''perception layer'' on real-world deployment, we provide a preliminary operational impact analysis. Considering the average power consumption of an NVIDIA H100 during active LLM inference, our GRU model's MAE of 7.97 W represents a very low relative error compared to the total power envelope. In the context of QoS-aware green scheduling, this high precision is expected to facilitate efficient ''slack distribution'' [55], where forecasting errors are naturally absorbed by the inherent scheduling gaps and performance buffers of the HPC cluster. Given that industrial SLAs for real-time workflows incorporate a slack allocation to manage task criticality and system jitter, the minimal latency overhead potentially introduced by forecasting inaccuracies remains well within standard operational margins. This suggests that energy-saving policies, such as proactive DVFS or task reallocation, can be applied without violating mandatory SLA constraints, effectively contributing to bridging the gap between high-performance inference and environmental sustainability. As a result, this paper will be a valuable resource for researchers and practitioners in the field of LLM serving, helping to guide future sustainable developments and applications. For future studies, we plan to investigate the feasibility of applying our approach to other largescale computing applications and investigate the potential for using the presented system in conjunction with other energy efficiency techniques and AI methods, including reinforcement learning, which has gained significant attention in energy-efficient distributed AI systems but has yet to be applied to sustainable LLMs. Furthermore, future research will address the complex trade-off between energy efficiency and inference latency; while this was outside the current scope due to its high dependence on specific hardware interconnects, it is a critical next step for real-world deployment.
